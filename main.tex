\begin{abstract}
    Surface waves propagating against a ring current are observed experimentally and investigated theoretically. The ring current is generated in a square basin by a rotating disk mounted flush with the bottom. The same disk excites wave motion because its normal is slightly tilted relative to the vertical axis of rotation. An analytical theory is developed to determine the eigenmodes of surface waves in a cylindrical basin in the presence of an established, axisymmetric, steady ring current. Comparison with the experimental results shows good agreement for the eigenfrequencies and spatial structure of modes that reach the basin sidewalls and are thus not trapped by the current. The trapped modes predicted by the theory are not observed in our setup, likely because of the mode-excitation mechanism.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Surface waves undergo refraction and reflection as they propagate on a horizontally inhomogeneous surface current; see, for example, the review \cite{peregrine1976interaction} and the book \cite{buhler2009waves}. Refraction can lead to wave trapping, in which waves are confined to a particular region \cite{kenyon1971wave}. One example involves waves propagating against a jet current that is localized in the transverse horizontal direction \cite{lavrenov1998wave,holthuijsen1991effects}, resulting in wave focusing and increased wave steepness \cite{quilfen2018storm}.

In this work, we investigate wave refraction and trapping in a ring current. The current is generated experimentally in a closed basin by a rotating disk mounted flush with the basin bottom at its center \cite{yang2019axisymmetric,piva2005steady}. The same disk excites waves because its normal is slightly tilted relative to the vertical axis of rotation. We consider only waves propagating against the current, which therefore tend to be trapped by it. The fluid flow, including both the current and wave motion, is measured at the free surface. In the shallow-water regime, wave refraction and trapping can also be achieved using a guiding ridge on the bottom \cite{sepulveda2020observation}. Our experiments, however, are in the deep-water regime, where this mechanism is absent.

To interpret the experimental data and validate our observations, we develop a theory of surface wave eigenmodes. The experimental basin is square, whereas the theory assumes an axisymmetric geometry. Nevertheless, we obtain reasonable agreement with the experimental data for modes that are predominantly localized near the maximum azimuthal velocity within the vortex and thus have relatively small amplitudes near the basin sidewalls. The theory assumes that the surface current is vertically uniform. This is the case for a ring current generated by a rotating disk \cite{yang2019axisymmetric}, except within a relatively thin boundary layer near the bottom. Vertical homogeneity is enforced by the Coriolis force, in accordance with the Taylor–Proudman theorem \cite{davidson2024dynamics}. The boundary layer is not essential to the theory because the observed surface wave modes are in the deep-water regime. The radial profile of the azimuthal velocity includes an inner core in solid-body rotation. The velocity reaches its maximum at the edge of the core, whose radius is approximately half the basin radius in our geometry. Beyond the core, the velocity decreases toward the sidewall. Secondary flows are relatively weak and are neglected in the theory.

General approaches have been developed to describe wave propagation on a background current in the linear regime, in the limit where the current velocity gradients are small compared with the wave frequency; see \cite{vergeles2024role,vergeles2025nonlinear} or \cite{mcwilliams2022quasi}. For our problem, however, a more effective approach is that developed in \cite{shrira2014trapped,shrira2014nonlinear} for a rectilinear geometry, which directly describes waves propagating against a straight jet current \cite{lavrenov1998wave,holthuijsen1991effects}. When the current is vertically uniform, an exact closed wave equation can be derived in terms of a single field, such as the pressure field. This approach also applies to cylindrical geometry.

Wave propagation on a background vortex was considered in \cite{torres2018waves}. The vortex was generated by a source distributed along a vertical axis, and its azimuthal and radial velocity components decreased inversely with distance from the source. Such a vortex does not support radially trapped waves, although the presence of a quasi-bound mode enhances the amplitude of a scattered wave. By contrast, our theory predicts trapped modes whose amplitudes decrease exponentially toward the sidewall. We observe qualitative and even quantitative agreement between theory and experiment for delocalized modes, which reach the sidewall and are reflected by it. However, we do not observe any trapped modes experimentally, although their parameters, such as frequency and azimuthal mode number, do not differ substantially from those of neighboring delocalized modes. We attribute this absence to the mode-excitation mechanism, which appears to require an interaction between the flow oscillations induced by the rotating tilted disk and the flow boundaries.

We describe the experimental setup in Section~\ref{sec:experimental-setup} and develop the theory in Section~\ref{sec:theory}. We compare the experimental and analytical results in Section~\ref{sec:results} and present brief conclusions in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.



\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:experimental-setup}

A square basin with dimensions $70\times 70 \times 10\,\text{cm}$ is filled with distilled water to a depth of $D = 10\,\text{cm}$. The basin sidewalls are made of glass. A disk of radius $30\,\text{cm}$ is mounted flush with the basin bottom at its center. A drive rotates the disk about a vertical axis at a fixed angular velocity. A schematic of the experimental setup is shown in the inset of Figure~\ref{fig:U-profiles}.

The horizontal components of the fluid velocity at the free surface, $v^x$ and $v^y$, are measured using particle image velocimetry (PIV). Polyamide PA-12 particles are scattered over the free surface. A camera mounted above the center of the basin records the horizontal motion of the particles. For each experiment, a video of duration $T=175\,\text{s}$ is recorded at a frame rate of $24\,\text{Hz}$.

Ten approximately uniformly spaced angular velocities in the range $0.024 ... 0.3\,\text{Hz}$ were tested. In each case, the radial profile of the azimuthal velocity $U$ was measured in a polar coordinate system $\{r,\varphi\}$ centered on the basin. All profiles were found to coincide after their amplitudes were normalized by the disk rotation speed. This indicates that the mean flow responds linearly and can be considered laminar. The profiles $U(r)$, both averaged over the azimuthal angle $\varphi$ and measured along selected directions, are plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:U-profiles}. In what follows, we consider only the experiment with the highest disk rotation speed, $\Omega \approx 0.31\,\text{Hz}$. For pure water, the Reynolds number characterizing the mean flow is $\mathrm{Re} = 40 000$, using a characteristic velocity of $25\,\text{cm}/\text{s}$ and a characteristic length of $15\,\text{cm}$.

Our setup is similar to that studied in \cite{yang2019axisymmetric}, differing only in the basin geometry: our basin is square, whereas that in \cite{yang2019axisymmetric} is cylindrical. Despite this difference, the radial profiles $U(r)$ agree well. In particular, the core in which the fluid undergoes solid-body rotation has a radius of \hbox{$\approx 14\,\text{cm}$} in our setup, approximately half the disk radius. This ratio is consistent with the $U(r)$ profiles in \cite{yang2019axisymmetric}, where the position of the maximum of $U(r)$ was found to depend on the aspect ratio $D/R$. The angular velocity $U/r$ reaches its maximum $\Omega$ at the center and decreases slightly toward the core edge, also consistent with the observations in \cite{yang2019axisymmetric}. We did not measure the vertical profile of the azimuthal velocity, but we expect it to be uniform except within the thin boundary layer near the bottom, based on the measurements in~\cite{yang2019axisymmetric}.

The deformation of the fluid surface due to centrifugal forces is relatively small. To estimate the difference in surface elevation between the edge and the center, we use Euler's equation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:N-S}
    \partial_t {\bm v} + ({\bm v}\nabla){\bm v} = - \nabla P_{\mathrm{total}},
\end{equation}
where the water density is set to unity and the physical pressure is $P_{\mathrm{total}} = P - gz$. For an axisymmetric, steady flow, the radial component of Euler's equation (\ref{eq:N-S}) is
\begin{equation}
    \partial_r P - U^2/r = 0. 
\end{equation}
The boundary condition at the fluid surface $z=H(r)$ is $P = gH$, since the physical pressure $P_{\mathrm{total}} = 0$ after subtracting the constant atmospheric pressure. For a given $U(r)$, the pressure solution gives
\begin{equation}
    H(r) = H(0) 
    + \frac{1}{g}\int\limits_0^r \mathrm{d}r^\prime \frac{U^2(r^\prime)}{r^\prime},
    \qquad 
    \int\limits_0^R Hr\mathrm{d}r = 0.
\end{equation}
The integral condition expresses conservation of the total fluid volume. For the profile shown in Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}, the difference is $H(R) - H(0) \approx 8\,\text{mm}$, assuming $R=47\,\text{cm}$. This is approximately one-twelfth of the water depth~$D$.

The boundary layer generates secondary flows in the radial and vertical directions \cite{yang2019axisymmetric}. These flows are weak compared with the azimuthal flow. In our setup, the measured mean radial velocity at the surface is also relatively small, $U^r/U^\varphi\sim 0.1$. It is not axisymmetric but instead follows the symmetry of the square basin.

The disk rotation also excites waves because the disk plane is tilted by $1^\circ$ from the horizontal, whereas the rotation axis is aligned with the vertical to greater precision. The rotating disk thus periodically perturbs the fluid motion. The measured frequency spectrum of the velocity field shows an appreciable response only at integer multiples of $\Omega$; see Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}. The peaks are narrow, so nonlinear peak broadening can be neglected and the system can be assumed to be in the linear-response regime. The peak amplitude does not vary monotonically with frequency. This is because the more intense overtones of $\Omega$ lie closer to resonant modes of the system than the weaker overtones, as discussed below.

\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

We assume an axisymmetric basin of radius $R$ and depth $D$. We introduce cylindrical coordinates $\{r,\varphi,z\}$, with $z=0$ at the unperturbed fluid surface. The ring current is steady and axisymmetric, ${\bm U} = U(r) {\textbf e}_\varphi$, where ${\textbf e}_\varphi$ is the azimuthal unit vector. We decompose the total fluid velocity ${\bm v}$ into the current velocity and the wave velocity ${\bm u}$, ${\bm v} = {\bm U}+{\bm u}$. Because we are primarily interested in waves with a relatively short characteristic wavelength $\lambda$, $\lambda/2\pi \lesssim D$, we neglect the vertical variation of the mean azimuthal velocity $U^\varphi$ within the boundary layer near the bottom. We also neglect the small radial and vertical components of the mean flow.

Euler's equation (\ref{eq:N-S}), linearized with respect to the surface wave amplitude, and the continuity equation are
\begin{equation}\label{linearized:001}
    \partial_t {\bm u}
    +
    ({\bm U} \nabla) {\bm u}
    +
    ({\bm u} \nabla){\bm U}
    =
    -\nabla p,
    \qquad 
    \mathop{\mathrm{div}}{\bm u} = 0.
\end{equation}
The boundary conditions are $u^z = 0$ at the bottom and $u^r=0$ at the sidewall. We write the free surface as $z = H + h$, where $h$ is the wave-induced displacement. The kinematic boundary condition at the free surface is
\begin{equation}\label{BC:kinematic}
    \partial_t h + \big({\bm U}\vert_{z=0} \nabla\big) h 
    = 
    u^z\big\vert_{z=0}.
\end{equation}
The dynamic boundary condition is
\begin{equation}\label{BC:dynamic}
    p + h \partial_z P = gh.
\end{equation}
The term $h\partial_z P$ vanishes because the mean flow is assumed to be vertically uniform. We neglect capillary effects because the characteristic wavelength lies in the gravity-wave regime.

For the mode analysis, we consider a mode with frequency $\omega$ and azimuthal mode number $m$, so that all wave fields $p, u^r, u^\varphi, u^z$ are proportional to $\exp(im\varphi - i \omega t)$. The components of Euler's equation (\ref{linearized:001}) are then
\begin{equation}\label{u_r_init}
    \begin{aligned}
        -i\widetilde \omega u_r - 2(U/r)u_\varphi &= -\partial_r p,
        \\[5pt]
        -i\widetilde \omega u_\varphi + 
            \big(2(U/r) + \Sigma\big) u_r &
            = -i(m/r)\,p,
        \\[5pt]
        -i\widetilde \omega u_z &= -\partial_z p,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\widetilde \omega(r) = \omega - m U/r$ is the Doppler-shifted frequency and $\Sigma = r \partial_r (U/r)$ is the local shear rate of the mean flow. The continuity equation is
\begin{equation}\label{continuity-eq}
    \frac{\partial_r(r u_r)}{r} +\frac{im u_\varphi}{r} + \partial_z u_z 
    = 0.
\end{equation}
The linearized Euler equations (\ref{u_r_init}) express all velocity components in terms of the pressure $p$:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    u_z = & \,-\frac{i}{\widetilde\omega }\,\partial_z p,
    \qquad 
    u_r = \frac{i}{\Lambda}
        \left(
        \widetilde\omega\,\partial_r - \frac{2mU}{r^2}
    \right)p,
    \\[5pt]
    u_\varphi &= \frac{1}{\Lambda }\, \left(
        \left(\frac{2U}{r}+\Sigma\right)\partial_r -\frac{m\widetilde \omega}{r}
    \right)p.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Substituting these expressions into the continuity equation (\ref{continuity-eq}) gives
\begin{equation}\label{p_eq_init}
    \frac{\widetilde\omega^2}{r}\left(\partial_r
        \frac{r}{\Lambda}\partial_r 
        -
    \frac{m(m+2\Sigma/\widetilde \omega)}{r\Lambda}
        +
    \frac{2mU\Lambda^\prime}{r\widetilde \omega \Lambda^2}
    \right)p 
    +
    \partial_z^2 p = 0,    
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda = \widetilde \omega^2 - 2(U/r)(2U/r+\Sigma)$ and the prime denotes differentiation with respect to the radial coordinate $r$. We consider only waves propagating against the current, so $mU<0$, with $\omega$ sufficiently large. Under these conditions, neither the shifted frequency $\widetilde\omega$ nor $\Lambda$ vanishes, and equation (\ref{p_eq_init}) is regular everywhere. A singularity would correspond to an instability. Experimentally, we observe a continuous spectrum in the range $mU>0$. Its amplitude is moderate, allowing us to neglect nonlinear interactions between waves with $mU<0$ and $mU>0$.

% TODO: \partial_r p = 0 != u_r = 0 on sidewall
The boundary conditions are $\partial_z p =0$ at the bottom and $\partial_rp=0$ at the sidewall. At the free surface, the kinematic boundary condition (\ref{BC:kinematic}) becomes $-i\widetilde\omega h = u^z $. Combining this condition with the dynamic boundary condition (\ref{BC:dynamic}) and the vertical component of Euler's equation (\ref{u_r_init}) gives the boundary condition for $p$ at $z=0$:
\begin{equation}\label{BC-for-p:free-surafec}
    \big( g \partial_z - \widetilde\omega^2\big) p 
    =
    0.
\end{equation}

In the absence of a mean flow, the wave equation (\ref{p_eq_init}) reduces, after separation of variables, to Bessel's equation of order $m$. Assuming $\partial_z^2 p = k^2 p$, we obtain $p_k = {\mathrm J}_m(kr)\cosh(k(z+D))$. The allowed wavenumbers $k$ are determined by the sidewall boundary condition, ${\mathrm J}^\prime_m(kR) = 0$. The free-surface boundary condition (\ref{BC-for-p:free-surafec}) determines the wave frequency $\omega$.

In the presence of a mean flow, equation (\ref{p_eq_init}), together with the conditions at the rigid boundaries, can still be solved by separation of variables. However, the free-surface boundary condition (\ref{BC-for-p:free-surafec}) now depends on the radial coordinate, so the full problem does not admit separation of variables.

We solve the full problem numerically. We determine the first $N$ eigenfunctions ${\mathcal F}_i(r,z)$ and wavenumbers $k_i$ of equation (\ref{p_eq_init}), subject to the bottom and sidewall boundary conditions. The action of the linear operator $\hat {\mathcal B} = g \partial_z - \widetilde\omega^2$ from (\ref{BC-for-p:free-surafec}) on an eigenfunction at the surface is $\hat {\mathcal B} {\mathcal F}_i = B(k_i){\mathcal F}_i$, where $B(k_i) = g\tanh(k_iD) - \widetilde\omega^2$. We then construct an orthonormal basis ${\mathcal J}_i(r)$ from the first $N$ solutions of (\ref{p_eq_init}) for $U=0$, evaluated at the surface. The inner product uses the integration measure $r\mathrm{d}r$ for the radial coordinate $r$. We expand the solution as
\begin{equation}
    p = \sum_{i=1}^N A_i {\mathcal F}_i.
\end{equation}
In this basis, the free-surface boundary condition (\ref{BC-for-p:free-surafec}) becomes
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i=1}^N B_{ji}A_i = 0,
    \qquad 
    B_{ji}
    = 
    \big\langle {\mathcal J}_j \big\vert B(k_i)\, {\mathcal F}_i(z=0)\big\rangle.
\end{equation}
A nontrivial solution exists only if the matrix is singular, $\mathop{\mathrm{det}}|B_{ij}| = 0$. The determinant is a real function of the frequency $\omega$, so errors due to the finite cutoff $N$ in the sums only slightly shift a solution. For our purposes, $N=15$ was sufficient. We denote each solution by $\omega_{m,n}$, where the positive integer $n$ increases with eigenfrequency. Once the frequency $\omega_{m,n}$ is found, the coefficients $A_i$ are given by an eigenvector of the matrix~$B_{ji}$ with zero eigenvalue.

The results of the numerical procedure are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:map}. Plus signs and crosses in the $m$-$\omega$ plane denote the calculated eigenmodes. The solutions form curved, approximately horizontal lines. Each line corresponds to a fixed radial mode number $n$, which counts the radial half-waves. The lowest-frequency modes at fixed $m$ have $n=1$. In these modes, the velocity component $u_\varphi$ vanishes only at $r=0$. In other modes, $u_\varphi$ has $n-1$ additional zeros within the interval $r<R$. Examples of radial eigenmode profiles are plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:wave-theory}.

We consider waves in the deep-water regime for which capillary effects remain negligible. The characteristic wavelength $\lambda$ must therefore lie in the interval $4\,\text{cm} < \lambda < 60\,\text{cm}$, where the lower bound corresponds to the minimum group velocity of capillary-gravity waves and the upper bound is $2\pi D$. To estimate the radial dependence of the wave field, we use the WKB approximation in the short-wavelength limit. The local dispersion relation is $\widetilde \omega^2  = gk$. The squared wavenumber is $k^2 = m^2/r^2 + k_r^2$, where $k_r$ is the radial wavenumber. Thus, the relation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kr}
    k_r^2 (r;\omega,m)
    =
    \frac{\widetilde\omega^4}{g^2} - \frac{m^2}{r^2}
\end{equation}
describes the radial oscillations of the mode velocity field. Toward the vortex center, $k_r$ vanishes at some $r=r_l$ because of the centrifugal force represented by the last term in (\ref{eq:kr}). If the ring current is absent or sufficiently weak, $k_r$ remains real for all $r>r_l$. The wave therefore reaches the sidewall and is reflected by it. If the current is sufficiently strong, $k_r$ vanishes again at some $r_r > r_l$. In this case, the wave is expected to be trapped (i.e., localized) within the ring current. The light gray region in Figure~\ref{fig:map} indicates where the point $r_r$ exists. However, the potential well must be sufficiently deep for localization to occur, so the gray region slightly overestimates the range in which localized modes exist. The modes denoted by crosses are indeed localized, with amplitudes that decay exponentially toward the sidewall.

\section{Comparison of the experimental data and the theoretical results}
\label{sec:results}

Now we proceed to compare our experimental observations with the theoretical predictions. One should remember that the theory considered the basin to be circle whereas it is square in experiment, so the mode matching should be expected to be only qualitative.

The experimentally measured modes are forced oscillations, so their frequencies are multiples $l\Omega$ of the rotation angular velocity $\Omega$ of the disc, see spectrum in Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}. In Figure~\ref{fig:modes-exp}, the radial profiles $|u_\varphi|$ for selected modes are plotted. Along the radial coordinate $r$, the measurements were carried till a full circle or radius $r$ can be placed into the basin, that is $r<35\,\text{cm}$. We plot the wave fields only up to radius $32\,\text{cm}$ due to the presence of a turbulent boundary layer of thickness $\approx 3\,\text{cm}$ near the side walls, where the measurements contain too large error. For example, the matching for the mode $(m,n) = (3,3)$ is rather good in Figure~\ref{fig:modes-exp}a and is quantitative. We note that the first node of the theoretically calculated profile is slightly closer to the basin center than than the node of the experimentally measured profile. The rule is fulfilled for others low-$n=2,3$ modes at different $m$ as well, see e.g. Figure~\ref{fig:modes-exp}f. This is the reason why the corresponding $n$ are chosen in the case of higher radial numbers $n$, see Figures~\ref{fig:modes-exp}b,d, although here one has rather qualitative agreement instead of a quantitative one.

The closer frequency $l\Omega$ is to the eigenfrequency of the mode, the higher is its amplitude. The relative amplitudes of the measured modes are given in Figure~\ref{fig:modes-exp} which allows one to evaluate the proximity to a resonance. In Figure~\ref{fig:map}, intensities of all wave fields measured at fixed $m$ and $l$ are depicted by colored filled circles. The matching is depicted by curved lines connecting theoretically calculated modes and experimentally observed wave fields.

Ones we have established the mode matching, we tried to observe those modes which are localized in radial direction by the mean flow. According to Figure~\ref{fig:map}, the lowest axial number of interest in the case is $m=7$. However, the mode was not detected, see Figure~\ref{fig:modes-exp}e. Instead, some extremely delocalized surface oscillations with relatively low amplitude was observed. This observation cannot be explained by relatively high value of $m$, since the mode $(7,2)$ is well observed, see Figure~\ref{fig:modes-exp}f. The same story is for $m=8$; the radial profile of theoretically calculated mode (8,1) is plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:wave-theory}. We make a conclusion that the localized modes are not excited in our experiment. As the distinctive feature of localized modes is the absence of interaction with the side walls, we make an inference that the chosen way of the excitation of the oscillatory motion essentially includes the fluid interaction with the side walls.

When $\sqrt{m^2+n^2}\gtrsim 9$, the nodes in the experimentally measured profiles are typically less pronounced, so a comparison with the theoretical predictions becomes ambiguous. Possibly, this is due to limiting accuracy of our measurements or simultaneous excitation of more than one eigenmode. As these wave numbers correspond to rather short waves for which the capillary effects are notable we are not interesting in these modes here. 


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Surface waves travelling against a ring current established in a closed basin of square shape are experimentally observed.  The forcing of the surface ways is produced due to the disk generating the ring current has a small tilt. The observations are approved by a systematic comparison with the numerical results obtained by the means of developed theory. The theory predicts that both delocalized and trapped modes should be presented among the modes excited in the experiment. However, only delocalized mode were detected experimentally. Suggested explanation argues to the trapped modes do not interact with the side walls of the basin, so they are not excited under the chosen way of surface wave motion forcing. This are questions to be experimentally explored what modes will be excited in a basin of cylindrical shape and how to excite trapped modes.
